### Topic: `receiving` — "Receiving & Storage" 📦

**1. Approved Suppliers: Safety Starts Before the Truck Arrives**
- **t:** Food safety starts with who you buy from, before any delivery hits
  the dock. An approved supplier sources from inspected facilities, follows
  safe transport practices, and can produce paperwork proving it. Buying
  from an unapproved source puts a hazard in your kitchen that no amount of
  careful cooking downstream can fully undo.
- **example:** A manager is offered a case of "discount" chicken from
  someone selling out of a cooler in their trunk — no invoice, no temp log,
  half the usual price. It might be fine. There's no way to know, and
  that's the problem: no paperwork means no chain of custody. She declines
  and orders from the approved distributor instead. Nobody sees that
  decision, but it's the biggest food-safety call of the day.
- **bullets:**
  - Approved suppliers source from facilities regulated by USDA/FDA/local authority
  - Reputable suppliers can show invoices, temperature logs, and safety certifications on request
  - Never accept food from unmarked vehicles, unlicensed vendors, or personal/home sources
  - Shellfish and other specialty items need supplier certification numbers specific to that product
  - Supplier approval is ongoing, not a one-time check — reputable suppliers welcome scrutiny
- **why:** Every safety control that follows receiving assumes the food
  started out wholesome. Skip the approved-supplier check and every
  downstream safeguard is left compensating for a hazard that may already be
  baked in and invisible.
- **memory:** 🧠 SOURCING RULE: No paperwork, no deal — no matter the price.
- **tip:** The exam frames this as a "who do you buy from" scenario. Any
  answer describing an unlicensed, unmarked, or undocumented source is
  always wrong, even when the food "looks fine."

**2. The Receiving Inspection Routine**
- **t:** Receiving is a checkpoint, not a formality. Inspect every delivery
  immediately on arrival — don't leave it on the dock, sign for it
  sight-unseen, or wheel it straight into storage.
- **example:** A kitchen has deliveries dropped at a side door whenever the
  driver shows up, mid-lunch-rush. Boxes sit unopened for 40 minutes because
  no one has time to check them. TCS food sits at ambient temperature the
  entire time, and by the time someone gets to it, no one can tell how long
  it was out. Compare that to a prep cook who schedules deliveries for a
  slower stretch, checks the invoice against the pallet, temps the dairy,
  and only then signs and moves everything into storage — cold items first.
- **bullets:**
  - Schedule deliveries for slower periods and assign a specific person to receive them
  - Check temperatures FIRST for any TCS item, before checking anything else
  - Compare the invoice/purchase order against what actually arrived
  - Inspect packaging integrity; move cold/frozen items into storage before dry goods
  - Document and reject on the spot — don't accept now and sort it out later
- **why:** Once the driver leaves, your leverage to reject or replace unsafe
  product is gone. A delayed inspection also means TCS food may have already
  spent unmonitored time in the danger zone before anyone opens the box.
- **memory:** 🧠 RECEIVING ORDER: Temp check first, then packaging, then
  invoice match. Cold/frozen into storage before dry goods sit around.
- **tip:** If a scenario says food "sat on the dock" or was "signed for
  without inspection," that's the flaw the question wants you to identify.

**3. Receiving Temperatures: The Numbers You Must Know Cold**
- **t:** Every food category has a specific temperature it must be at when
  it arrives. These numbers are heavily tested because a receiving-
  temperature failure means every later safeguard is built on food that was
  already in trouble.
- **example:** Cold TCS food (dairy, cut produce, deli meat): 41°F or below.
  Hot TCS food (rotisserie chicken): 135°F or above. Live shellstock, shell
  eggs, and milk get a narrow allowance of 45°F at the door — but shellstock
  and milk must still reach 41°F within 4 hours of receiving. That 45°F
  window isn't a looser rule; it's a brief, time-limited exception that
  still ends at the same 41°F standard as everything else.
- **bullets:**
  - Cold TCS food: 41°F or below at receipt — no exceptions
  - Hot TCS food: 135°F or above at receipt
  - Frozen food: solid, no signs of thawing and refreezing
  - Live shellstock: 45°F air temp at receipt, cooled to 41°F within 4 hours, tags kept 90 days
  - Shell eggs: 45°F air temp or below at receipt
  - Milk: 45°F or below at receipt, cooled to 41°F within 4 hours
- **why:** The 45°F allowance exists because shellstock, eggs, and milk
  have industry-recognized handling standards that account for brief transit
  variance — as long as the kitchen finishes the job by cooling to 41°F
  quickly.
- **memory:** 🧠 RECEIVING TEMPS: Cold TCS ≤41°F. Hot TCS ≥135°F. Frozen =
  solid. Shellstock/Eggs/Milk ≤45°F at the door only. Shellstock tags = keep
  90 days.
- **tip:** The exam loves the 41°F vs. 45°F distinction as a trap. General
  cold TCS food = 41°F. Shellstock/eggs/milk specifically at the moment of
  delivery = 45°F acceptable — never as a storage temperature.

**4. Rejecting a Delivery: Red Flags You Must Never Accept**
- **t:** Knowing when to reject is just as important as knowing the correct
  temperatures. Some problems are about the thermometer; others are about
  what your eyes, nose, and hands can detect.
- **example:** A case of frozen shrimp arrives with large ice crystals
  inside the bag and solid ice fused to the bottom — signs it thawed and
  got refrozen. It reads frozen right now. Reject it anyway. A case of
  canned tomatoes has a few cans with bulging ends — a possible sign of
  gas-producing bacteria inside, a botulism red flag you can't taste or
  smell. The dent alone is the whole answer.
- **bullets:**
  - Reject frozen food with large ice crystals, solid ice buildup, or liquid staining
  - Reject cans that are swollen, dented on a seam, leaking, or rusted through
  - Reject any delivery showing pest evidence: droppings, gnaw marks, chewed packaging
  - Reject food with off odors, off colors, slimy/tacky texture, or damaged packaging
  - Reject TCS food that fails its receiving temperature check — don't accept and "fix it later"
- **why:** A can that looks intact but is swollen can harbor Clostridium
  botulinum toxin — one of the most dangerous foodborne hazards there is,
  and cooking may not destroy it. Some hazards never show up on a
  thermometer at all.
- **memory:** 🧠 AUTO-REJECT LIST: Thaw-refreeze signs, damaged/swollen cans,
  pest evidence, off odor/color/texture, failed TCS temp. Any ONE = reject.
- **tip:** Watch for scenarios where the temperature is correct but there's
  another red flag. Correct temperature doesn't automatically mean accept —
  it's one of several independent pass/fail checks.

**5. Dry Storage: Temperature, Humidity, and Airflow**
- **t:** "Shelf-stable" doesn't mean "ignore the environment." Poor dry
  storage conditions shorten shelf life, invite pests, and can cause
  moisture-related spoilage even in food that never needed refrigeration.
- **example:** A dry storage room sits at 85°F next to a hot water heater,
  boxes stacked directly on the floor. Humidity is high enough that flour
  has clumped and cardboard has gone soft at the bottom. A jug of degreaser
  sits on the shelf above a case of canned beans. Every one of these is a
  separate, correctable failure.
- **bullets:**
  - Ideal dry storage temperature: 50°F to 70°F
  - Ideal relative humidity: 50% to 60%
  - Store all food at least 6 inches off the floor, on shelving, away from walls
  - Keep dry storage clean, dry, and pest-free with good air circulation
  - Store chemicals separately from food, always below/away from it, never above
- **why:** The 6-inch rule lets air circulate underneath stored food, keeps
  mopping from splashing product, and denies pests hidden, undisturbed space
  against a wall or floor.
- **memory:** 🧠 DRY STORAGE: 50–70°F, 50–60% humidity, 6 inches off the
  floor, FIFO always.
- **tip:** "6 inches off the floor" is a near-guaranteed exam question,
  often paired with boxes stacked directly on tile. It applies in dry
  storage, walk-ins, and freezers alike.

**6. Cold and Frozen Storage: Protecting the Cold Chain**
- **t:** Once TCS food passes receiving, the job shifts to keeping it cold
  without interruption — the "cold chain." Every break, even a short one
  from a propped-open door or a blocked fan, gives bacteria an opening.
- **example:** A walk-in cooler is packed so tight that boxes block the fan.
  Cold air can't circulate, so shelves near the door run in the upper 40s
  while the log — checked only at the back wall — says 38°F. Nobody knows
  the front shelves are out of spec, because the one reading taken looked
  fine.
- **bullets:**
  - Cold storage: 41°F or below at all times
  - Frozen storage: cold enough to keep food solid, generally at or near 0°F
  - Never overload shelves or block fans/vents — blocked airflow creates warm pockets
  - Cover and label all stored food to prevent contamination and moisture loss
  - Check and log cooler/freezer temperatures regularly with a calibrated thermometer
- **why:** A single thermometer reading only tells you the temperature at
  that one spot. Blocked airflow can leave other shelves several degrees
  warmer without anyone knowing.
- **memory:** 🧠 COLD CHAIN: 41°F or below, no interruptions. Check the
  warmest spot, not just the easiest spot.
- **tip:** Watch for an overstuffed walk-in or a thermometer reading fine
  while food near the door feels warm. The fix is reorganizing for airflow
  and checking multiple locations, not trusting one reading.

**7. Storage Order in the Cooler: Top to Bottom by Cook Temperature**
- **t:** Where you place food in a cooler is a deliberate defense against
  drip cross-contamination. The rule: stack from the LOWEST required cook
  temperature on top to the HIGHEST on the bottom.
- **example:** Ready-to-eat food on top never gets cooked again, so it must
  never risk drips from anything below. Poultry — the highest-risk item at
  165°F — goes on the very bottom, so any drip from it only ever falls onto
  the floor of the cooler, never onto food that won't get hot enough to
  neutralize what just landed on it.
- **bullets:**
  - Top shelf: ready-to-eat food (never cooked again)
  - Next: seafood (145°F minimum)
  - Next: whole cuts of beef and pork (145°F minimum)
  - Next: ground meats (155°F minimum)
  - Bottom shelf: whole and ground poultry (165°F minimum — highest risk)
- **why:** This ordering assumes the worst case — a container leaks. A
  food's own minimum cook temperature tells you how much heat it would take
  to neutralize whatever lands on it, so the shelf order tracks that number
  exactly.
- **memory:** 🧠 SHELF ORDER (top→bottom): RTE → Seafood → Whole Beef/Pork →
  Ground Meats → Poultry. Cook temp climbs as you go down: none, 145, 145,
  155, 165.
- **tip:** One of the most frequently tested visual/scenario questions on
  the exam. Poultry on the bottom and ready-to-eat on top are the two
  anchors — everything else falls in between by cook temperature.

**8. Date Marking and FIFO: Tracking Food Through Storage**
- **t:** Date marking tracks the age of ready-to-eat TCS food held more
  than 24 hours, so nobody has to guess whether something in the walk-in is
  still safe. FIFO rotation makes sure the oldest labeled food actually
  gets used first.
- **example:** A cook makes pasta salad on Monday and stores it at 41°F.
  Monday counts as Day 1. Counting forward (Tue 2, Wed 3... Sun 7), it must
  be used or discarded by the end of the following Sunday — not the
  following Monday, which would be Day 8. A new batch made Thursday goes
  BEHIND Monday's batch on the shelf, so staff reach the older batch first.
- **bullets:**
  - Date marking applies to ready-to-eat TCS food held longer than 24 hours
  - Maximum storage: 7 days at 41°F or below, counting the prep/open day as Day 1
  - Labels must show the food name and the discard (use-by) date, not just the prep date
  - Food past its discard date must be thrown out regardless of how it looks or smells
  - FIFO: newer stock goes behind or under older stock
- **why:** Listeria monocytogenes grows slowly but steadily even at proper
  refrigeration temperatures, with no visible warning sign. The 7-day limit
  isn't about visible spoilage — it's a hard ceiling on how long
  refrigeration alone can be trusted to control that slow, silent growth.
- **memory:** 🧠 DATE MARKING: RTE TCS food held over 24 hrs → date mark it.
  Prep/open day = Day 1. Max 7 days at 41°F. FIFO always puts new stock
  behind old stock.
- **tip:** The exam gives a prep date and asks for the discard date.
  Remember the prep day IS Day 1 — a Monday prep date discards at the end
  of the following Sunday, not Monday.

### Topic: `haccp` — "HACCP & Active Managerial Control" 📋

**1. HACCP: A Proactive System — and When You Need a Variance**
- **t:** HACCP (Hazard Analysis Critical Control Point) builds safety into
  the process itself instead of inspecting the finished product after the
  fact — developed in the 1960s for the space program, where a sick
  astronaut in orbit was never an option. Certain high-risk processes go a
  step further: the operator must get a variance from the regulatory
  authority AND submit a written HACCP plan before starting them.
- **example:** Smoking wings for flavor, then finishing on the grill, needs
  no variance. Smoking salmon AS the method of preservation — shelf-stable
  smoked jerky, not a flavor step — does. That's the trap: read whether
  smoking is a flavor step or the entire preservation method. Serving raw
  oysters or a rare burger is a separate rule entirely — it needs a written
  consumer advisory, not a variance, and is never allowed for highly
  susceptible populations.
- **bullets:**
  - Triggers: smoking-to-preserve, curing, additive/vinegar preservation
    that removes TCS status, reduced oxygen packaging (vacuum, sous vide,
    cook-chill) outside narrow exemptions, shellfish display tanks,
    on-site sprouting, custom-processing an owner's own animal food
  - The regulatory authority must review and approve the plan before the process may begin
  - Running one of these without an approved variance is a violation on its own, even with no illness
  - Raw/undercooked animal food service needs a consumer advisory, not a variance
- **why:** Each triggered process removes one of food's natural safety
  barriers — oxygen, refrigeration, or a kill step — and replaces it with a
  process-specific control. A vacuum-sealed bag removes the oxygen that
  slows spoilage bacteria but does nothing to stop Clostridium botulinum,
  which thrives without oxygen.
- **memory:** 🧠 VARIANCE NEEDED FOR: Smoking-to-preserve, Curing, ROP/Sous
  Vide, Shellfish Tanks, Sprouting, Custom-Processing an owner's animal food.
- **tip:** The exam loves the smoking trap — flavor step vs. entire
  preservation method.

**2. The 7 HACCP Principles, In Order**
- **t:** HACCP is a 7-step sequence, and the order isn't arbitrary — you
  can't set a critical limit before you know your control points, and you
  can't monitor a limit that hasn't been established yet.
- **example:** Ground beef chili, walked through all seven: (1) hazard
  analysis — raw beef may carry E. coli and Salmonella; (2) CCP — cooking
  eliminates it; (3) critical limit — 155°F for 15 seconds; (4) monitoring —
  the cook checks internal temp every batch; (5) corrective action — a
  batch reading 145°F keeps cooking until it hits 155°F/15 sec; (6)
  verification — the manager reviews logs weekly; (7) record-keeping — the
  temperature log stays on file for the inspector.
- **bullets:**
  - 1. Conduct a hazard analysis
  - 2. Determine critical control points (CCPs)
  - 3. Establish critical limits
  - 4. Establish monitoring procedures
  - 5. Establish corrective actions
  - 6. Establish verification procedures
  - 7. Establish record-keeping
- **why:** Skipping a step or doing them out of order leaves gaps — a plan
  that jumps straight to monitoring without identifying CCPs ends up
  watching the wrong thing.
- **memory:** 🧠 7 PRINCIPLES: Analyze → Assign (CCPs) → Limit → Watch →
  Fix → Confirm → Document.
- **tip:** If a question describes what to DO when a limit is missed,
  that's Principle 5 (corrective action) — not Principle 4 (monitoring is
  only the checking).

**3. Critical Control Points vs. Control Points**
- **t:** A control point influences a hazard, but a later step can still
  fix it if control slips. A critical control point (CCP) is the LAST point
  where a hazard can be prevented, eliminated, or reduced to safe — lose
  control there and nothing downstream saves the food.
- **example:** Receiving chicken at 41°F is a control point — slightly warm
  can still be corrected by cooking later. Storage at 41°F is a control
  point — it slows growth but isn't the final barrier. Cooking to 165°F is
  a CCP — the kill step; miss it and nothing later fixes it. Hot holding at
  135°F is also a CCP — drop below it and surviving bacteria multiply again
  with no kill step left before service.
- **bullets:**
  - CCP = the last point where a hazard can be prevented, eliminated, or reduced to safe
  - Control point = influences a hazard but isn't the final safety barrier
  - Cooking, hot holding, and cold holding are the classic CCPs
  - Receiving is typically a control point, not a CCP
  - A critical limit is always measurable — never a vague standard
- **why:** Regulators and operators focus monitoring resources on CCPs
  because that's where a mistake actually reaches the customer.
- **memory:** 🧠 CCP TEST: "If this step fails, can ANY later step fix it?"
  No → it's a CCP.
- **tip:** Look for the step tied to a specific number — a temperature or a
  time. "Check the delivery" is usually a control point, not the CCP being tested.

**4. Monitoring and Corrective Actions**
- **t:** A critical limit means nothing if nobody checks whether it's being
  met. Monitoring must answer four questions: what, how, when/how often,
  and who. Corrective action is what happens the instant a limit is missed.
- **example:** A cook-chill soup line's plan specifies: WHAT — soup temp;
  HOW — calibrated thermocouple in the geometric center; WHEN — end of
  cooking and every 2 hours during hot holding; WHO — the shift lead. At the
  2-hour check, the soup reads 125°F. Corrective action: determine how long
  it's been below 135°F; if under 4 cumulative hours, reheat to 165°F and
  return to holding; if the time can't be determined or exceeds 4 hours,
  discard. Then record the deviation and check why the steam table wasn't holding.
- **bullets:**
  - Monitoring must specify WHAT, HOW, WHEN/how often, and WHO
  - Monitoring should be frequent enough to catch a deviation before food reaches the customer
  - Corrective action fixes the immediate problem — reheat or discard the affected food
  - Corrective action also addresses the root cause so it doesn't repeat
  - Every corrective action must be documented
- **why:** Monitoring without a corrective-action plan tells you food is
  unsafe without telling anyone what to do about it. The two work as a pair.
- **memory:** 🧠 MONITORING = WHAT, HOW, WHEN, WHO. CORRECTIVE ACTION = FIX
  THE FOOD + FIX THE CAUSE + RECORD IT.
- **tip:** If a scenario asks for the correct corrective action, look for
  the option that addresses the CURRENT food immediately, not just future prevention.

**5. Active Managerial Control: The Five CDC Risk Factors**
- **t:** Active Managerial Control (AMC) means building ongoing systems
  that prevent illness before it happens. The CDC and FDA trace the
  overwhelming majority of foodborne illness outbreaks back to five risk factors.
- **example:** A restaurant practicing AMC doesn't wait for a complaint to
  discover the ice machine hasn't been cleaned in months — a written
  cleaning schedule already caught it.
- **bullets:**
  - The 5 risk factors: unsafe sources, inadequate cooking, improper
    holding temps, contaminated equipment/surfaces, poor personal hygiene
  - AMC control for each: approved-supplier checks, standardized recipes
    with temp checks, scheduled holding checks, written cleaning schedules,
    handwashing + illness-exclusion enforcement
  - AMC is proactive — built into daily procedures, not triggered by an
    inspection or illness
  - The PIC is responsible for demonstrating and maintaining AMC every shift
- **why:** Outbreak root causes trace back to the same five risk factors
  again and again. Focusing management attention there converts food safety
  from luck into process.
- **memory:** 🧠 5 RISK FACTORS: Unsafe Source, Inadequate Cooking, Improper
  Holding, Contaminated Equipment, Poor Hygiene.
- **tip:** A manager following a schedule = AMC. A manager scrambling to fix
  something only after a complaint = reactive, not AMC.

**6. Responding to a Suspected Foodborne Illness Complaint**
- **t:** When a customer or employee reports symptoms consistent with
  foodborne illness, the PIC's response in the first hours matters. The core
  response is always: preserve, document, notify, cooperate.
- **example:** A customer calls the morning after dining, reporting
  vomiting and diarrhea that started 14 hours after the meal, with two
  companions reporting the same. The instinct is to clean everything up
  immediately — don't. Preserve any suspect food still in the building, log
  the complaint in writing, notify the regulatory authority, and cooperate
  fully with any request for samples or interviews.
- **bullets:**
  - Preserve suspect food — do not discard, cook, or serve it
  - Log every complaint: names, foods eaten, onset time, symptoms
  - Notify the regulatory authority promptly
  - Cooperate fully with the health department's investigation
  - Review staff health status for any Big 5 illness requiring exclusion
  - Big 5 pathogens requiring mandatory reporting: Norovirus, Salmonella
    Typhi, Shigella, STEC, Hepatitis A
- **why:** Discarding suspect food out of a desire to "clean up" destroys
  the exact evidence that could clear the kitchen — or confirm the real cause.
- **memory:** 🧠 OUTBREAK RESPONSE: Preserve the food, Log the complaint,
  Notify the health authority, Cooperate with the investigation.
- **tip:** Any answer involving discarding suspect food or staying quiet
  about a complaint is always wrong.

**7. Imminent Health Hazards: Fire, Flood, Sewage, Power Loss, Equipment Failure**
- **t:** An imminent health hazard is a significant, immediate threat that
  requires the operation to stop right away. The sequence is always the
  same: stop, protect, notify, wait for approval.
- **example:** The power blinks off for 20 minutes. It feels minor, but the
  process is identical regardless of size — assess affected food/water/
  utilities, keep fridge/freezer doors closed, discard TCS food once it's
  been above 41°F for 4+ cumulative hours, and notify the regulatory
  authority. You don't get to privately decide it wasn't a big deal and
  skip that step.
- **bullets:**
  - Recognized triggers: fire, flood/sewage backup, extended power outage,
    water service interruption, refrigeration failure
  - Step 1: STOP the affected operation immediately
  - Step 2: PROTECT — discard compromised food, safeguard what's still safe
  - Step 3: NOTIFY the regulatory authority
  - Step 4: WAIT for approval before reopening the affected operation
- **why:** These events can compromise food safety even when the food looks
  and smells normal — sewage-contaminated food shows no visible sign, and a
  failed refrigerator can sit unsafe for hours before anyone notices.
- **memory:** 🧠 IMMINENT HAZARD = STOP, PROTECT, NOTIFY, WAIT FOR APPROVAL.
  Never reopen on your own judgment alone.
- **tip:** The correct process is the same no matter how minor the crisis
  seems — the exam tests whether you skip notification because it "wasn't a big deal."

**8. Regulatory Inspections and Food Safety Training Requirements**
- **t:** Health inspections verify that day-to-day practices match Food
  Code requirements. Violations get classified by actual risk, which
  determines how fast they must be corrected.
- **example:** An inspector finds a sanitizer bucket testing at 0 ppm
  (a PRIORITY item, corrected on the spot), a cracked cutting board (a
  PRIORITY FOUNDATION item, replaced within the correction window), and a
  missing handwashing sign (a CORE item, corrected within a longer
  timeframe). None rise to an imminent health hazard, so the restaurant
  keeps operating. If sewage were backing up into the prep sink instead,
  the regulatory authority could suspend the permit and close the
  establishment immediately.
- **bullets:**
  - The regulatory authority has the right of entry to inspect at reasonable times, without notice
  - The PIC should accompany the inspector and provide access to requested records
  - Priority items require the fastest correction; priority foundation and core items get longer windows
  - An imminent health hazard found during inspection can mean immediate closure
  - Every food employee needs basic food safety knowledge; most jurisdictions require at least one certified food protection manager on staff
- **why:** Classifying violations by actual risk lets both inspector and
  operator focus urgency where it belongs, instead of treating a missing
  sign the same as a critical-limit failure.
- **memory:** 🧠 INSPECTION: Regulatory authority has right of entry. PIC
  cooperates. Priority items fix fastest. Imminent hazard = close now, reopen only with approval.
- **tip:** Don't confuse a routine violation (correction window, stays
  open) with an imminent hazard (immediate closure, needs approval to reopen).

### Topic: `facilities` — "Facilities & Equipment" 🔧

**1. Equipment Materials & Standards**
- **t:** Not every surface can safely touch food. Food-contact surfaces
  must be smooth, nonabsorbent, durable, and easily cleanable — an
  NSF/ANSI mark means the equipment was tested against commercial sanitation
  design standards.
- **example:** A manager brings a beautiful raw wood cutting board from
  home to sit next to the kitchen's scarred-but-intact NSF-certified poly
  board. The wood looks nicer, but its microscopic pores soak up moisture
  and juices that no amount of scrubbing removes. Separately, a cook stores
  acidic tomato sauce overnight in a galvanized steel pan — the acid reacts
  with the zinc coating and leaches metal into the sauce, a chemical
  contamination hazard.
- **bullets:**
  - Food-contact surfaces: smooth, nonabsorbent, durable, corrosion-resistant, easily cleanable
  - An NSF/ANSI mark means the equipment was tested against commercial sanitation design standards
  - Raw/unsealed wood is never acceptable as a food-contact surface
  - Galvanized (zinc-coated) metal must never touch acidic food
  - Scratched, pitted, or cracked surfaces must be resurfaced or replaced
- **why:** A surface that looks clean isn't the same as a surface that CAN
  be cleaned. Pores, scratches, and seams trap food residue below where a
  sponge can reach.
- **memory:** 🧠 MATERIALS: Smooth, nonabsorbent, durable, cleanable. Look
  for NSF/ANSI. No raw wood, no galvanized metal with acid.
- **tip:** Think about porosity (does it absorb moisture/bacteria?) and
  reactivity (does it react with acid?) before answering a materials question.

**2. Equipment Installation: Legs, Seals, and Space to Clean**
- **t:** Equipment needs the right material AND the right installation, so
  every surface — including the floor and wall behind and beneath it — can
  actually be cleaned.
- **example:** A bakery's floor mixer sits flush on the tile with no legs
  and no seal. Flour dust and moisture collect in the gap where the base
  meets the floor — nobody can sweep under it. Six months later, an
  inspection finds a mouse nest back there. The fix: install the mixer on
  6-inch legs, so a mop head fits underneath and the area stays visible
  during daily cleaning.
- **bullets:**
  - Floor-mounted equipment: sealed to the floor OR 6+ inches of leg clearance
  - Tabletop equipment: sealed to the table OR 4+ inches of clearance
  - The goal either way: eliminate a hidden gap that can't be cleaned or inspected
  - Equipment should allow access to all sides, above, below, and behind for cleaning
  - Easily movable equipment is exempt as long as it's actually moved regularly for cleaning
- **why:** An inaccessible gap is invisible during daily cleaning and often
  during inspection too. Pests seek out exactly these undisturbed,
  debris-rich spaces.
- **memory:** 🧠 INSTALLATION: Floor-mounted = 6 inches. Tabletop = 4
  inches. No usable gap = no cleaning = pest magnet.
- **tip:** The exam may swap the numbers or offer a close-but-wrong option
  like 2 or 8 inches — know which number goes with which equipment type.

**3. Handwashing Sinks: Dedicated, Stocked, and Always Accessible**
- **t:** A handwashing sink has exactly one job. The moment it gets used
  for anything else, it stops being a hygiene tool and becomes a
  contamination source.
- **example:** A short-staffed kitchen has only one sink within reach of
  the line — the handwashing sink. One cook rinses lettuce in it, another
  dumps ice into it, a third fills a mop bucket from it. By dinner rush,
  anyone who "washes their hands" there is getting recontaminated by the
  sink itself.
- **bullets:**
  - Handwashing sinks: ONLY for handwashing — never food prep, thawing, or dumping anything
  - Must be conveniently located in prep, service, warewashing, and restroom areas — never blocked
  - Required at every sink: soap, warm running water, a drying method, a waste container
  - A posted sign reminding employees to wash their hands is required
  - An out-of-service handwashing sink is an immediate fix, not a "later" problem
- **why:** Every barrier between a food handler and the handwashing sink —
  occupied, missing soap, three rooms away — pushes them toward a shortcut.
- **memory:** 🧠 HANDWASH SINK = Handwashing ONLY. Stocked with soap + warm
  water + towels/dryer + trash can + sign. Never blocked, never multi-purpose.
- **tip:** Any scenario where a handwashing sink gets used for rinsing
  vegetables or filling a pot is always a violation, regardless of how
  clean the rinsed item was.

**4. Plumbing, Cross-Connections, and Backflow Prevention**
- **t:** A cross-connection is any physical link between the safe drinking
  water supply and a contamination source. A drop in water pressure can
  reverse normal flow — backflow — pulling whatever sits at the outlet
  backward into the pipes that supply the whole building.
- **example:** A porter leaves a hose end submerged in a bucket of
  sanitizer solution while he steps away. A nearby pressure drop briefly
  reverses flow, pulling diluted sanitizer back into the pipes that feed
  the handwashing sinks and ice machine down the line.
- **bullets:**
  - A cross-connection links the potable water supply to a contamination source
  - An air gap must be at least 2× the diameter of the water supply outlet, never less than 1 inch
  - A vacuum breaker serves the same purpose where a physical air gap isn't practical
  - Never leave a hose end submerged in a sink, bucket, or mop water
  - Grease traps catch fats/oils/grease before they reach the sewer system
- **why:** Backflow is dangerous specifically because it's invisible and
  brief — the contamination event can last seconds during a pressure drop,
  then the system looks completely normal again.
- **memory:** 🧠 AIR GAP = 2× the outlet diameter, never less than 1 inch.
  No air gap possible? Vacuum breaker. Never submerge a hose end.
- **tip:** Memorize "2 times the diameter." Watch for scenarios describing
  a hose left in a bucket — a cross-connection violation even if nothing
  bad has happened yet.

**5. The Water Supply: Potable, Pressurized, and Reliable**
- **t:** Every drop of water used — cooking, ice, handwashing, warewashing,
  cleaning — has to start from a source that's safe to drink. Beyond the
  source, the plumbing has to deliver hot and cold running water under
  enough pressure to actually be usable.
- **example:** A roadside diner draws water from a private well the
  previous owner installed decades ago. Never tested. It looks and tastes
  fine — but "fine" isn't a food-safety standard, since human senses can't
  detect most water contamination.
- **bullets:**
  - Water must come from an approved public system or a properly tested private well
  - Hot and cold running water must be available under adequate pressure at every required fixture
  - A boil-water advisory means tap water can't be assumed safe until it's lifted
  - Private well water should be tested periodically, not just once at installation
  - Weak or absent hot water at a required fixture must be corrected, not worked around
- **why:** Potability can't be judged by taste or smell — that's why the
  standard is about the source, not how the water presents.
- **memory:** 🧠 WATER SUPPLY = Approved source + Hot AND cold + Running +
  Under pressure. Boil-water advisory = stop assuming tap water is safe.
- **tip:** If a scenario mentions a private well, check whether it's been
  tested — an untested well is the giveaway, even if the water "looks fine."

**6. Lighting Intensity**
- **t:** Light works as a food-safety control, not just comfort. Minimum
  light intensity, measured in foot-candles, is set by the risk level of
  the tasks performed in each area.
- **example:** A walk-in cooler lit by one weak bulb (about 5-6
  foot-candles, well under the 10 fc minimum) means a prep cook can't read
  a date label clearly enough to catch that the tomatoes are two days
  expired. A prep table with a meat slicer lit at 30 fc, under the 50 fc
  minimum for bladed equipment, threatens both food safety and the
  employee's fingers.
- **bullets:**
  - 50 foot-candles minimum: knives, slicers, grinders, saws
  - 20 foot-candles minimum: handwashing/warewashing areas, self-service areas
  - 10 foot-candles minimum: walk-ins, dry storage, dining areas
  - Light fixtures over food areas must be shielded or shatter-resistant
- **why:** The tiers track task risk: cutting with a blade needs the
  sharpest vision, reading labels needs solid visibility, navigating a
  storage room needs the least.
- **memory:** 🧠 LIGHTING: 50 fc = Knives/Slicers/Prep. 20 fc =
  Handwash/Warewash/Self-Service. 10 fc = Walk-in/Dry Storage/Dining.
- **tip:** Memorize the pattern 50-20-10 tied to task risk. Identify the
  area type first, then apply the number.

**7. Ventilation and Hood Systems**
- **t:** Cooking produces heat, steam, smoke, and grease-laden vapor that
  need somewhere to go besides settling back onto food and surfaces.
  Exhaust hoods capture and remove it before it becomes a contamination or
  fire problem.
- **example:** A restaurant's exhaust hood filters haven't been cleaned in
  months. Grease builds up thick enough that vapor drifts sideways across
  the cook line instead of venting up, leaving a film on nearby prep
  surfaces — and creating a serious fire hazard inside the hood itself.
- **bullets:**
  - Exhaust hoods remove grease-laden vapor, heat, steam, and smoke
  - Grease filters must be cleaned on a regular schedule — buildup is both
    a contamination and a fire hazard
  - Exhaust air should vent outside, never recirculate back into prep or dining areas
  - Condensation from any overhead source must never drip onto food or food-contact surfaces
- **why:** Grease-laden vapor that isn't captured doesn't disappear — it
  settles as a sticky film on every nearby surface and, over time,
  accumulates into a serious fire risk inside the hood system.
- **memory:** 🧠 VENTILATION = Remove grease/heat/steam/smoke, vent
  OUTSIDE. Dirty hood filters = fire hazard, not just a cleaning issue.
- **tip:** Grease film on surfaces or condensation dripping onto food both
  point to ventilation system failure — the fix is hood/exhaust
  maintenance, not just wiping down the surface.

**8. Floors, Walls, Garbage, Restrooms & Pest-Proof Design**
- **t:** The building itself is food-safety equipment. Floors and walls in
  food areas need to be smooth, durable, and cleanable — and how you
  contain garbage and design transitions shapes whether a kitchen stays
  pest-free.
- **example:** An older kitchen has a sharp 90-degree corner where the
  floor meets the wall. Grease and moisture collect there because a mop
  can't reach into a tight right angle. A renovated kitchen has coving — a
  rounded, sealed transition — and the same corner wipes clean in one pass.
  Separately, outdoor garbage containers missing lids sit against the back
  door, overflowing after a busy weekend — attracting pests with a short
  trip straight into the building.
- **bullets:**
  - Floors, walls, and ceilings in food areas: smooth, durable, easily cleanable
  - Coving at floor-wall joints eliminates the hard-to-clean 90-degree corner
  - Garbage containers: durable, leak-proof, covered, emptied often enough to prevent overflow
  - Restrooms: conveniently located, self-closing door, handwashing sink
  - Every unsealed gap around pipes, walls, and doors is a potential pest entry point
- **why:** Pests and bacteria exploit the same weakness — places hard to
  see, hard to reach, and hard to clean. Designing the gap out beats
  cleaning around it forever.
- **memory:** 🧠 FACILITY DESIGN = Smooth/durable/cleanable surfaces +
  Coving + Covered leak-proof garbage + Restrooms with self-closing doors + No unsealed gaps.
- **tip:** Coving questions test whether you understand WHY it matters —
  eliminating a hard-to-clean corner where debris accumulates, not just that it exists.
